<p>Below are the key elements and indicators used in options trading. Understanding these will help you better interpret option contracts and market data.</p><h2>Contract Elements of Options</h2><p>Options come in two types: <strong>Call Options</strong> and <strong>Put Options</strong>.</p><p>Apart from direction, each option contract consists of the following core components:</p><h3>1. Strike Price</h3><p>The predetermined transaction price of the underlying asset that applies when the option buyer decides to exercise the contract.</p><h3>2. Expiry Date</h3><p>The contract’s expiration date.</p><p>Options lose value over time and <strong>expire</strong> after this date.</p><ul><li>In the U.S. market, regular stock options usually expire <strong>every Friday (except when the market is closed—then expiration moves to Thursday).</strong></li><li>Monthly options expire on the <strong>third Friday</strong> of each month.</li><li>Index options may have <strong>mid-week expirations</strong> in addition to Friday expiries.</li></ul><p><strong>American-style options</strong> → Can be exercised <strong>any time before expiry</strong></p><p><strong>European-style options</strong> → Can be exercised <strong>only on the expiry date</strong></p><h3>3. Contract Multiplier</h3><p>The multiplier used to determine the contract’s total value based on the strike price.</p><p>Example:</p><p>If the multiplier = <strong>100</strong>, and the strike price = <strong>20</strong> →</p><p>Contract value = <strong>100 × 20 = 2,000</strong></p><h3>4. Contract Size</h3><p>The actual quantity of the underlying asset each option contract represents.</p><p>Typically <strong>1 option = 100 shares</strong>, unless adjusted due to corporate actions.</p><p>Example:</p><p>Option premium = <strong>0.50</strong></p><p>Contract size = <strong>100</strong></p><p>Total premium = <strong>0.50 × 100 = 50</strong></p><p> </p><h2>Option Indicators and Greeks</h2><p>Option Greeks measure how option prices react to changes in market conditions.</p><h3>1. Implied Volatility (IV)</h3><p>The market’s expectation of the underlying asset’s future volatility.</p><p>IV is derived by inputting the option’s market price into a pricing model (commonly the <strong>Black-Scholes model</strong>).</p><h3>2. Delta</h3><p>Measures how much the option price changes when the underlying asset moves by one unit.</p><p>Example:</p><p>Delta = 0.50 → If the underlying rises by $1, the option price increases by ~$0.50.</p><h3>3. Gamma</h3><p>Measures how much <strong>Delta</strong> changes when the underlying asset moves by one unit.</p><p>Gamma reflects the sensitivity of Delta.</p><h3>4. Theta</h3><p>Measures <strong>time decay</strong> — how much the option price decreases as one day passes, assuming all else stays constant.</p><h3>5. Vega</h3><p>Measures how much the option price changes when the underlying asset’s volatility increases by <strong>1%</strong>.</p><h3>6. Rho</h3><p>Measures how much the option price changes when the <strong>risk-free interest rate</strong> changes by <strong>1%</strong>.</p><p> </p><p style="margin-left: 0.0px"><strong>Disclosures</strong></p><p style="margin-left: 0.0px"><i>This article is for reference only and does not constitute any investment advice.</i></p>
