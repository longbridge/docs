<p style="margin-left: 0cm">Long-term orders remain valid until the specified date, but their execution may be affected by corporate actions. Here’s how it works.</p><h3>1. What is a long-term order?</h3><p>Long-term order is an order type that remains in effect until a specified date unless the transaction has been fulfilled or cancelled. If the trade is not executed, the long-term order will be cancelled after the end of the specified trading day. Long term orders include &#34;GOOD TILL DAY (GTD)&#34; and &#34; GOOD TILL CANCELLED (GTC) &#34; orders.</p><h3>2. Will corporate actions have an impact on the execution of long-term orders?</h3><p>Whether a corporate action will affect the execution of a long-term order mainly depends on if the corporate action will result in a change with customer's shareholding, such as share consolidation and share split. Please refer to the following examples:</p><figure class="table"><table><colgroup><col style="width: 15.53%"/><col style="width: 25.34%"/><col style="width: 59.13%"/></colgroup><tbody><tr><td colspan="1" rowspan="1"><p style="text-align: center">Share Consolidation</p></td><td colspan="1" rowspan="1"><p>Reduce the number of shares outstanding and consolidate existing shares.</p><p>(Par value of each stock ↑ Shareholders' shareholding ↓ Market value , total shareholders' equity remain unchanged)</p></td><td colspan="1" rowspan="1"><p>If the customer holds 100 shares of company A and the current stock price is $5, the value of the stock held is 100*5=$500.</p><p>If Company A merges 10 shares into 1 share, the shareholder holds 100/10=10 shares after the merger, the stock price will be 5*10=$50; The value of the shares held is 10*50=$500.</p><p>If the customer buys 100 shares before the merger, he needs $500; yet, it requires $5000 for the same purchase of 100 shares after the merger.</p><p>Due to the changes in the number of shares and market prices after the merger, long-term orders will not be able to take effect.</p></td></tr><tr><td colspan="1" rowspan="1"><p style="text-align: center">Share Split</p></td><td colspan="1" rowspan="1"><p>Increase the number of shares outstanding and spin off existing shares.</p><p>(Par value of each stock ↓ Shareholders' shareholding ↑ Market value , total shareholders' equity remain unchanged)</p></td><td colspan="1" rowspan="1"><p>If the customer holds 100 shares of company A and the current stock price is $5, the value of the stock held is 100*5=$500.</p><p>If Company A split from 1 shares into 10 share, the shareholder holds 100*10=1000 shares after the splitting, the stock price will be $5/10 = $0.5 ; The value of the shares held is 1000*0.5=$500.</p><p>If the customer sells 100 shares at $5 before the stock split, that equals $500; yet, if the 100 shares are sold after the stock split, only 1/10 of the original volume will be sold.</p><p>Due to the changes in the number of shares and market prices after the merger, long-term orders will not be able to take effect.</p></td></tr></tbody></table></figure><p>From here we can see that this type of corporate action will lead to an impact on long-term orders.</p><h3>3. If I have issued a long-term order before the Ex-date of relevant corporate action, how will Longbridge handle such a situation?</h3><p>The long-term order will be cancelled <strong>before the US market opens on the Ex-date</strong> of the corporate action. Please re-issue the order if necessary.</p><p> </p><p style="margin-left: 0cm"><strong>Disclosures</strong></p><p style="margin-left: 0cm"><i>This article is for reference only and does not constitute any investment advice.</i></p>
