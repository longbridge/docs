<p style="margin-left: 0cm">This article provides a detailed introduction to American Depositary Receipts (ADRs) and the custody fees associated with them.</p><h3 style="margin-left: 0cm">1. Introduction to ADRs</h3><p style="margin-left: 0cm">An ADR is a negotiable certificate issued by a US commercial bank that allows foreign securities to be traded in the US. You can trade an ADR on the US stock market. Each ADR represents a certain number of shares of a non-US company. These shares are held by a US bank, which then issues the ADRs representing the shares. The conversion ratio between the company's shares and the ADR is not always 1:1. For example, five shares of the company might be represented by a single ADR.</p><h3 style="margin-left: 0cm">2. How custody fees are charged</h3><p style="margin-left: 0cm">The Depository Trust Company (DTC) began charging custody fees to brokers or clearing firms in 2008. These brokers or clearing firms then pass the fee on to investors holding ADRs. This is known as the ADR custody fee.</p><h3 style="margin-left: 0cm">3. ADR custody fee</h3><p style="margin-left: 0cm">The custody fee for ADRs averages between 1 to 5 cents per share. The exact amount charged and the timing of the charge can vary for different ADRs. For specific information, please refer to the prospectus of each ADR or query online using the <a href="https://www.sec.gov/edgar/searchedgar/companysearch.html">EDGAR company search tool</a>.</p><h3 style="margin-left: 0cm">4. Identifying ADR stocks</h3><p style="margin-left: 0cm">Generally, non-US companies with shares trading on US secondary markets may be subject to ADR custody fees.
